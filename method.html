How the 90 days work

Three stretches. Each one ends with something you can see in your house.

See where the hours go

Weeks 1–3

For seven days, you write down the minute you stop working and what pulls you back in. Pen and paper. No apps. Then you read it out loud with someone who won't flinch.

What changes: You know the real reason you're late. It's often not the workload.

Make 5:15 hold

Weeks 3–6

You run the 4:50 Protocol. 10 minutes to shut down at your desk. 15 minutes to commute and clear your head, ending with a 2-minute Driveway Reset. Home by the 5:15 Hard Stop.

You sit down with your wife over Saturday coffee and agree on how evenings work. At the office, you move meetings, cut repeat work, and hand off what someone else can carry.

What changes: Your calendar defends 5:15, and your wife has a reason to trust it.

Let the house hold the line

Weeks 6–12

At 5:15, your phone goes to one set spot, the same one every day. Your kids see it there and know Dad is home. You write down the proof from your own weeks. A monthly check-in catches drift before it comes back.

What changes: You stop needing willpower. The house does the work.

Graduation: Earning Systems Dad Status

The program doesn't just fizzle out. At the end, you write your family a letter. We get on a 30-minute call, I read it, and I check that your 4:50 Protocol held for 30 days.

"You held the line. You're in the photographs. The system is yours now. You're a Systems Dad."

Frequently Asked Questions

Honest answers for skeptical operators who have tried other systems.

We don't teach you to ignore real fires. We teach you to tell real fires from bad habits. In the first six weeks, you get the exact script for late meetings: "I have a hard stop at 5:15. Can we move this to 3:00 instead?" In almost every case, people move the call without an argument. If a true fire breaks out once a quarter, you stay on purpose. And you log it in your tracker as an intentional choice, not an accident.

If you give an emotional speech tonight, she will be skeptical. She should be. She has seen you burn out and slip back before. That's why you sit down with her over coffee and agree on the rules together. You don't make big promises. You acknowledge what she carried alone. You agree on three simple rules (Arrival, Phone, Bedtime). Then you set a 30-day review date. Words don't rebuild trust. The phone sitting on the charger at 5:15 rebuilds trust.

Productivity books teach you to do more in less time. That just dumps more work on your plate. The Systems Dad Method isn't about time management. It is about physical boundaries and clear roles. When your phone stays on the charger and your laptop stays out of the bedroom, you don't need willpower. The room does the work for you.

Distance is physical, but presence is a decision. When you are on the road, the 25-minute window is event-anchored before your hotel door or dinner. You block dedicated FaceTime check-ins with your kids—morning drop-off or bedtime—and protect them on your calendar like a client meeting. In the hotel room, the threshold holds: the laptop stays zipped in your bag, and the phone stays off the bed.

We don't pretend corporate emergencies never happen. But you run the Advance Notice rule: if a deadline pushes past 5:00, you text your wife by 3:00 PM so she isn't left guessing. The laptop closes at the office, never in the living room. You take two minutes in the driveway to flush work adrenaline. And your phone goes to its set spot the second you walk inside. Late work is an intentional exception, not a license to bring Slack to bed.
