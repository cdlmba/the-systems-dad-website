<p></p><p>You know that feeling, right? That relentless hum of &#8220;not enough.&#8221; Not enough hours, not enough productivity, not enough you to go around. Or as I like to say to my kids - &#8220;There&#8217;s only one Daddy.&#8221;</p><p>We&#8217;re told to hustle, to grind, to sprint our way to success. For years, I bought into it. My calendar was a battlefield, my to-do list a never-ending war, and my default state was exhaustion. I thought fifth gear was the only gear in the engine. I had to get to complete burnout, twice.</p><p>I was reminded of that old self at the <a href="https://www.northsidechristianchurch.net/">Kingdom Men conference at my church</a>. I went in expecting sermons on leadership and financial wisdom. What I got was a profound lesson on margin, velocity, and why our constant sprinting sabotages the very things we&#8217;re trying to achieve. <a href="https://www.thebravewayhome.com/">Ben Woods</a> was our speaker, sharing the following points:</p><ol><li><p>Holy Pursuit</p></li><li><p>Holy Pace</p></li><li><p>Holy Purpose</p></li></ol><p>The whole of the conference was amazing, but I couldn&#8217;t get past the pace part.</p><p>It wasn&#8217;t about doing more. It was about radically rethinking how we move through life. It&#8217;s about a fundamental reorientation of our lives, our work, and our homes that promises deep care, sustained mission, and a life truly lived, not just survived.</p><h2>The Modern Myth of the Relentless Sprint</h2><p>The current cultural narrative is completely broken. It&#8217;s the Gospel of <strong>More, Faster, Now</strong>.</p><p>Hustle culture is mainstream. We&#8217;re told success equals suffering, sleep equals weakness, and our worth is measured by productivity metrics. This culture rewards the appearance of busy over genuine, focused output.</p><p>The smartphone has become a digital leash. The boundaries between work and life are erased. This constant state of alert prevents the mind from ever truly resting.</p><p>Even amongst Christians, there&#8217;s an unspoken competition to look successful. To have a full calendar and a resume suggesting superhuman capability. This fear of being seen as lazy fuels an unsustainable pace.</p><p>This only speed-runs burnout. A state of emotional, physical, and mental exhaustion caused by prolonged stress, often accompanied by chronic anxiety that makes stillness unbearable.</p><p>When every ounce of energy pours into the sprint, there&#8217;s nothing left for the people who matter most. Time with family becomes transactional. Conversations are rushed. I wore my exhaustion as a badge of honor, checking email late at night and rising before dawn.</p><p>This pace didn&#8217;t produce holiness. It produced havoc. It led to health scares (like a panic attack), moments of deep cynicism, and an unsettling distance from the peace I claimed to preach.</p><h2>The Kingdom Men Reminder: Redefining Velocity</h2><p>My core principle of achieving velocity without burnout means we are aligning our lives with the Lord&#8217;s pace. This isn&#8217;t idleness, but a divinely ordained rhythm that sustains rather than depletes.</p><p>The world defines velocity as constant, high-speed movement leading to exhaustion. The Lord&#8217;s pace maintains movement and progress, but operates on grace, rest, and renewal. It&#8217;s the antithesis of back-to-back scheduling.</strong></p><ul><li><p>Intentionally block out hours with no assigned task for deep work, creative problem-solving, or emergent needs. Place 15-30 minute buffers between meetings to prevent delays from derailing your day. Don&#8217;t schedule to 100% capacity&#8212;operate at 70-80%, leaving 20-30% as strategic reserve.</p></li><li><p>Energy margin ensures your emotional and physical tank never runs on empty. It acknowledges that restoration is productive.</p></li><li><p><strong>Recognize depletion symptoms:</strong> irritability, reduced focus, excessive stimulants. Schedule sleep, exercise, and meals with the same status as major deadlines&#8212;these are operational necessities. Identify and eliminate energy leaks: doom-scrolling, chronic complaining, toxic relationships.</p></li></ul><p><strong>Financial margin removes the stress of living paycheck-to-paycheck. This directly impacts emotional and mental clarity.</strong></p><ul><li><p>A financial cushion allows decisions based on opportunity and values rather than necessity. It enables strategic risk and provides security to step away from toxic environments.</p></li></ul><p><strong>Emotional and mental margin is the most critical and hardest to maintain. It&#8217;s the capacity for stillness, self-awareness, and genuine connection.</strong></p><ul><li><p>Practice pausing to review actions, consider direction, and confirm alignment with core purpose. Allow time to digest difficult emotions and complex information without immediately moving to the next task. Be fully engaged in the current moment without racing ahead or dwelling on the past.</p></li></ul><p>Creating margin requires consistent defense against the pressures of busy culture.</p><ul><li><p><strong>Audit your 168 hours.</strong> Map out a typical week honestly. Identify commitments that can be delegated, postponed, or dropped.</p></li><li><p><strong>Define and defend boundaries.</strong> Treat your margin as appointments you can&#8217;t cancel. Learn to use &#8220;no&#8221; without guilt. Wrap your family inside your protected space.</p></li><li><p><strong>Batch and theme.</strong> Group similar tasks into specific time blocks. Use themed days to reduce context switching and protect focus time.</p></li><li><p><strong>Build in pauses.</strong> Schedule a 30-minute review weekly and a half-day reflection monthly to ensure you&#8217;re doing the right work.</p></li></ul><p><strong>Stop glorifying busy.</strong> Reject the narrative that equates exhaustion with importance. Measure success by quality of output and sustained well-being.</p><h2>The Courage to Be Led and Slowed Down</h2><p>The pursuit of a holy pace requires profound courage. It&#8217;s not the boldness to act, but the radical willingness to stop, wait, and be led into stillness and surrender.</p><p>Our society makes us feel perpetually inadequate and late. The pressure to attend every event and seize every opportunity breeds debilitating fear of being excluded or irrelevant.</p><p>The courage to choose a slower, quieter path demands confronting the lie that the best life is the fullest life. True fullness is found in alignment, not accumulation.</p><p>We must dismantle the idea that life is a competitive race. When we choose a God-directed pace, we&#8217;re not falling behind&#8212;we&#8217;re operating on a different clock. God&#8217;s metrics are directly opposed to the world&#8217;s metrics of speed and volume.</p><p>The core of this courage lies in shifting authority from our frantic ambition to God&#8217;s quiet, sovereign timing.</p><p>Life operates in two types of time: <em>chronos</em> (linear, clock time) and <em>kairos</em> (God&#8217;s opportune, perfect time). Personal ambition operates in Chronos, demanding action now. A holy pace submits to Kairos, resting in the belief that God orchestrates events perfectly.</p><blockquote><p>Learn to distinguish God&#8217;s gentle, life-giving invitations from culture&#8217;s urgent, soul-draining demands.</p></blockquote><p>God&#8217;s invitations bring peace, clarity, sustenance, and a sense of enough. They align with our core purpose and deepen intimacy with Him. Societal demands bring urgency, comparison, depletion, and the pursuit of external validation.</p><p>Theology without action is theory. The courage to slow down requires deliberate, practical choices.</p><ol><li><p><strong>The power of saying no.</strong> This is the ultimate act of self-leadership. Saying no to a good opportunity is often necessary to say a wholehearted yes to the best opportunity or necessary rest.</p></li><li><p><strong>The wisdom of delegation.</strong> Recognizing you&#8217;re not indispensable and empowering others is maturity, not weakness. Delegation clears mental and physical space.</p></li><li><p><strong>The practice of waiting and pausing.</strong> Waiting is a spiritual discipline that dismantles impatience and pride. Pausing before reacting creates a vital gap between stimulus and response, allowing the Holy Spirit to inform your next move.</p></li></ol><p>In a world that celebrates noise and ceaseless motion, stillness is a beautiful, radical protest.</p><p>Stillness is worship. To be still is to declare that God is God and you are not. It&#8217;s releasing the illusion of control and submitting to a higher, gentler authority. The holy pace is sustained not by strength of will, but by the lightness of surrender.</p><h2>Rest as a Weapon, Not a Weakness</h2><p>The relentless pace of modern life equates productivity with constant motion, viewing rest as a luxury or laziness. This misunderstanding is the fast track to burnout.</p><p>To achieve a holy pace, we must embrace deep, integrated rest. This goes far beyond pausing work&#8212;it&#8217;s about cultivating margin in our lives.</p><p>Many people rest through passive consumption: endless scrolling, binge-watching, mindless gaming. The constant pressure depletes the margin necessary for genuine family connection.</p><p>We must intentionally shift the home environment.
